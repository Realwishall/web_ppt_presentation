Magnetic Flux

Electric flux  — the pattern
ΦE = E ⋅ A = E A cos θ
Magnetic flux  — same idea, field B
ΦB = B ⋅ A = B A cos θ

Flux is the field's dot product with the area vector A (normal to the surface). Swap E for the magnetic field B and you have magnetic flux — measured in weber (Wb).

Why the angle matters

The cos θ story

  • θ = 0° — face-on.  Loop's normal is parallel to B, every line passes through. Flux is maximum: Φ = BA.
  • θ = 60°.  Loop is tilted, only cos 60° = ½ of the lines get through. Φ = BA/2.
  • θ = 90° — edge-on.  Loop's plane is parallel to B, no lines cross. Flux is zero.
θ = 0° cos θ = 1.00 → Φ = 1.00 Wb
θ = 30° cos θ = 0.87 → Φ = 0.87 Wb
θ = 45° cos θ = 0.71 → Φ = 0.71 Wb
θ = 60° cos θ = 0.50 → Φ = 0.50 Wb
θ = 90° cos θ = 0.00 → Φ = 0.00 Wb

Changing B, A, or θ changes the flux — and a changing flux is exactly what induces an EMF. That's the gateway to electromagnetic induction.

Recap

Key takeaways

  • Magnetic flux Φ = B·A·cos θ counts the field lines threading a surface.
  • SI unit is the weber (Wb), where 1 Wb = 1 T·m². It is a scalar.
  • Flux is maximum when the loop faces the field (θ=0°) and zero when edge-on (θ=90°).
  • A changing flux — via B, A or θ — drives Faraday's law of induction.

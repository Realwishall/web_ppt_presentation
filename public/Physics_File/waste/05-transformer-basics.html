Alternating Current · Part 5

The Transformer

A device that changes AC voltage and current, purely using a changing magnetic flux.

Why not just send 220 V down the line?

  • A consumer wants 220 V, 10 A — but the transmission line has resistance, so some voltage is lost as heat: ΔV=IR
  • Example: plant creates 320 V at 10 A, but only 220 V reaches the consumer — 100 V is lost in the wire
  • Power lost is I2R — since it depends on I2, the fix is to transmit at high voltage, low current, then step the voltage back down near the consumer
  • Sending the same power as V=22000 V, I=0.1 A means far less I2R loss for the same wire
This is the whole reason transformers exist in a power grid

The ideal transformer

  • Step-up: N2>N1 → raises voltage, lowers current
  • Step-down: N2<N1 → lowers voltage, raises current
  • Flux per turn is the same in primary and secondary (they share the same core) → V2V1=N2N1 — true even if efficiency isn't 100%
  • If no energy loss (ideal, η = 100%): V2V1=N2N1=I1I2

Worked examples

Question
Primary 100 turns, secondary 20 turns, input 220 V AC. Find output voltage.
V2=220×20100=44 V
Question
Same transformer, but supply is DC. What happens?
It will not work — DC gives a constant flux, so dΦ/dt=0 and no emf is induced in the secondary.
A transformer only ever works with a changing (AC) supply

Quick facts

  • Output frequency = input frequency — a transformer never changes frequency, only amplitude
  • Transformers have very high efficiency (~95–99%) because they have no moving parts — losses are only electrical/magnetic, not mechanical friction
Compare with a motor/generator, which does have moving parts and frictional loss

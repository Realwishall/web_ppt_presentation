What is Alternating Current?

Direct Current — DC

A DC current does not change its direction with time.

Alternating Current — AC

Direction of the current changes alternately with time.

Household mains is AC — it reverses direction 100 times a second (50 Hz).

Average & RMS values

Two "single numbers" that summarise a waveform over one cycle.

WaveformAverageRMS
DC — constant A AA
Sine — A sin(ωt) 2Aπ A2
Triangle — peak A A2 A3

Average of a full sine over one complete cycle is zero — these are the half-cycle / magnitude values used in problems.

RMS — special cases

Same ω  (AC + AC, same frequency)

y=Asin(ωt)+Bsin(ωt+60°)

Combine as phasors first (they add like vectors), then take RMS of the resultant amplitude.

Different ω  (AC + DC, or AC + AC of different ω)

y=A+Bsin(ωt)
y=Asin(ωt)+Bsin(2ωt+60°)

Add the squares of the individual RMS values: yrms=y12+y22

Skin Effect

The tendency of an alternating current (AC) to flow through the surface only of a conductor.

Most of the current flows near the surface.

  • For AC we use a hollow conductor — the core carries almost no current anyway.
  • To keep the total cross-section area the same, wires are used in bundle form.

Why does skin effect happen?

Assume the main current is increasing → its magnetic flux is increasing too (into the page ⊗).

Zone closer to the centre

Induced current opposes the main current's increasing nature.

Zone away from the centre

Induced current supports the main current's increasing nature.

Net effect: current is pushed outward toward the surface. Higher frequency → stronger skin effect.

Which is more dangerous?
  • In general DC is more dangerous — AC current takes zero value a hundred times a second
  • But in NCERT, for the same stated value, AC is more dangerous, as AC's peak value exceeds its written (RMS) value

The transmission problem

Send power over a long line of resistance R=10Ω.

Send  320V,10A
Voltage drop across line   =IR=10×10=100V
Delivered   =320−100= 220V

Raise the line to R=20Ω and you'd lose 200 V. High current on a resistive line wastes power as I2R. Solution: cut the current.

Transformer — the fix

Same power, but move it at high voltage / low current so I2R loss is tiny.

Step-up  (×100)

220V,10A → 22000V,0.1A

Sent along the line at low current.

Step-down  (×1/100)

22000V → 220V,10A

Delivered to the home, safe and usable.

Power P=VI is conserved; dropping current 100× cuts line loss by 10,000×.

Transformer losses (1 of 2)

Flux leakage

Issue: some flux doesn't link the secondary.

Fix: wind coils one over the other; or use diamagnetic material around the core to contain flux.

Copper loss

Issue: heating in the windings, I2R.

Fix: use wire of lower resistance (thicker / better conductor).

Transformer losses (2 of 2) — iron loss

Eddy-current loss

Issue: circulating currents in the core heat it up.

Fix: use a laminated core (thin insulated sheets).

Hysteresis loss

Issue: energy lost re-magnetising the core each cycle (B–H loop area).

Fix: use soft iron (thin loop). It may produce a humming noise.

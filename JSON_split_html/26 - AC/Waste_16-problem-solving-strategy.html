Alternating Current · Part 16

Solving AC Numericals — Strategy

A short checklist, plus the two simpler two-element circuits: RC and RL.

Before you touch a formula

  • First identify the type of supply — AC or DC?
  • A cell or battery is always a DC source — its frequency is zero
  • A capacitor is a perfect insulator for DC — it blocks DC completely once fully charged
  • All AC meters read the RMS value; a DC instrument will not respond correctly to an AC supply
  • If a data value is simply given with no label, assume it's RMS — not peak
  • If the frequency changes in a problem, remember to recompute X_L and X_C — they are not constants
  • Remember: Hz is the unit of frequency f, not of angular frequency ω (which is in rad/s)
  • All the phasor formulas here are for amplitude/RMS values, not instantaneous ones — for instantaneous values, just use plain KCL/KVL addition

RC and RL circuits (no third element)

RC circuit

Z=R2+XC2
Current leads voltage by some angle φ between 0° and 90°, tanφ=XC/R

RL circuit

Z=R2+XL2
Current lags voltage by some angle φ between 0° and 90°, tanφ=XL/R

Both are just the RLC formulas with one reactance term dropped

RL circuit — AC supply vs DC supply

  • Under a DC supply (steady state, f=0): XL=ωL=0 — the inductor behaves like a plain wire; current is limited by R alone: I=V/R
  • Under an AC supply: the inductor offers reactance XL=ωL, which grows with frequency — it impedes current more as frequency rises
  • So the same coil that's "invisible" on DC becomes an active current-limiter on AC — this coil, used purely for its reactance with negligible resistance, is called a choke coil

Differentiation: the mathematics of “right now”

Everything in physics happens now — this velocity, this force, this acceleration. The derivative is the tool that reads the present instant out of a changing quantity.

Checkpoints · 0 / 8
1.1

The feel of differentiation

Your trip computer reports average velocity — total displacement divided by total time. The speedometer reports the instantaneous value: what the average would be if the time window were squeezed down to nothing. That squeeze, \(\Delta t \to 0\), is the entire idea of the derivative.

  • Over a window, \(v_{avg}=\dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\) — geometrically, the slope of a secant line through two points of \(x(t)\).
  • Shrink the window: the secant pivots and settles onto the tangent line.
  • The limit is the derivative: \(v=\dfrac{dx}{dt}\) — the slope of position, right now.
Try it: drag \(\Delta t\) toward zero and watch the secant lock into the tangent. Move \(t_0\) to probe other instants, or hit Shrink Δt to watch the limit happen by itself.

B · Slope along a path

geometric view

The derivative is just slope, read off point by point. Slide the probe along \(f(x)=\tfrac{x^3}{3}-x\) and watch the tangent tilt; where the tangent goes flat, \(f'(x)=0\). Toggle the derivative curve — its height at any \(x\) is the slope you see on the left.

  • Positive slope → function climbing; negative → falling.
  • \(f'(x)=0\) at \(x=\pm1\): the crest and the valley (extrema).
x₀ — slope f′(x₀) — ≈ 0 — flat! extremum nearby

C · Maxima, minima & the perfect throw

optimization
θ — R — dR/dθ — —

At the top of an arc, the bottom of an energy well, the launch angle that throws farthest — the slope passes through zero. The first derivative finds candidates (\(f'=0\)); the second derivative referees: \(f''<0\) ⇒ the curve crests (maximum), \(f''>0\) ⇒ it bottoms out (minimum).

Projectile range is \(R(\theta)=\dfrac{v_0^{2}\sin 2\theta}{g}\). Setting \(\dfrac{dR}{d\theta}=\dfrac{2v_0^{2}\cos 2\theta}{g}=0\) forces \(2\theta=90^\circ\Rightarrow\theta=45^\circ\), and \(\dfrac{d^{2}R}{d\theta^{2}}<0\) there — a genuine maximum.

Watch the sign of dR/dθ: positive while \(R\) climbs, negative after it crests — the flip through zero is the first-derivative test happening live.

Integration: piling up the infinitely small

Physics keeps asking the other question: if little pieces accumulate continuously, what’s the total? Distance from a changing speed, energy from a changing force, charge from a changing current.

Checkpoints · 0 / 8
2.1

The feel of integration

Slice the interval into \(n\) pieces. Approximate each with a rectangle of height \(f(x)\) and width \(\Delta x\), and add them up. As \(n\to\infty\) the jagged tops dissolve into the curve, and the sum becomes the definite integral — exactly the area under the graph:

\[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x\]

  • Accumulation: an integral is a running total of tiny contributions \(f\,dx\).
  • Undoing: integration reverses differentiation — the Fundamental Theorem connects the area to an antiderivative.
  • Here \(f(x)=\dfrac{x(6-x)}{4}\) on \([0,6]\), whose exact area is exactly \(9\). Watch the sum converge.

2.2

The integration rulebook

Because integration undoes differentiation, every rule is the mirror image of one you already own. The new idea is the \(+C\): a derivative erases constants, so antiderivatives come in whole families — until physics hands you an initial condition.

Why \(+C\) is physics

initial conditions

Constant acceleration \(a=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) integrates to \(v(t)=2t+C\). Every value of \(C\) is a different physical situation with the same acceleration — the whole dashed family below. Only a measurement like \(v(0)=v_0\) pins \(C\) down. This is where “\(v_0\)” in every kinematics formula comes from: \(v(t)=at+v_0\).

\[\int a\,dt = at + C,\qquad v(0)=v_0 \Rightarrow C=v_0\]
C = v₀ — v(t) = 2t + C slope = 2 always
2.3

Area is physics

The same picture — area under a curve — is four different laws depending on which axes you chose. Four labs, one idea.

A · Work from F(x)

work–energy

Force acting over distance is work: \(W=\int F\,dx\). A spring pulls with \(F=kx\) — the area is a triangle, \(W=\tfrac12 kx^2\), which lands as kinetic energy. Release the 2 kg cart from rest and read off its launch speed.

W = ∫F dx — v = √(2W/m) —

B · Impulse from F(t)

momentum
J = ∫F dt — Δv = J/m — Favg = J/τ —

Force acting over time is impulse: \(J=\int F\,dt=\Delta p\). A short sharp crack and a long gentle shove can hand over the very same momentum — the collision only cares about the area. The pulse here is a half-sine, \(F(t)=F_0\sin(\pi t/\tau)\), with exact integral \(J=\dfrac{2F_0\tau}{\pi}\).

Fire the pulse and watch the area accumulate; the 1.5 kg cart’s \(\Delta v\) counts up with it.

C · Running kinematics backwards

v(t) → x(t)

Area under \(v(t)\) is displacement — signed area. Here \(v(t)=6-1.5t\): the particle surges forward, halts at \(t=4\), then reverses. By \(t=8\) the positive and negative areas cancel and it is back where it started — displacement zero, distance traveled 24 m. Press play and scrub.

D · Average value

the flat equivalent
∫₀ᵇ f dx — f̄ = 1/(b−a)·∫ —

Every varying quantity has a flat stand-in with the same total: \[\bar f=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\] The dashed line is the height of the rectangle with identical area. Slide \(b\) across a full period of the pure sine (\(2\pi\)) and the average collapses to zero — exactly why AC circuits quote RMS values instead.

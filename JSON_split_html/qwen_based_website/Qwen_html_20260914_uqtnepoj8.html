Before any graph

The sign convention — stated once

Cartesian rules

  1. Incident light travels left → right (+x).
  2. All distances are measured from the pole (mirror) or optical centre (lens).
  3. Along the incident-light direction → positive; opposite → negative.
  4. Heights above the axis → +ve; below → −ve.

⟹ The payoff

A real object always has u < 0. That single fact fixes which half of the hyperbola your answer must live on.

Lens formula

1v − 1u = 1f , m = vu

Mirror formula

1v + 1u = 1f , m = − vu

Slide 9 · The method

The 4-step recipe for any v–u graph

Both asymptotes come from one trick: make a quantity blow up.

  1. Put v = ∞, solve for u

    The image is at infinity ⟹ the object sits at the focus. This gives the vertical asymptote.

  2. Put u = ∞, solve for v

    Object at infinity ⟹ image forms at the focus. This gives the horizontal asymptote.

  3. Shift the origin to where the two asymptotes cross

    That crossing point (u₀ , v₀) is the new origin. Draw the four new quadrant lines through it.

  4. Draw the rectangular hyperbola through (0, 0) and the diagonally opposite zone

    The curve always passes through the true origin, and its two branches occupy diagonally opposite quadrants of the new origin.

Which two opposite zones?

Lens: (u+f)(v−f) = −f² ⟹ zones II & IV of the new origin, branches rising.
Mirror: (u−f)(v−f) = +f² ⟹ zones I & III, branches falling.

⚠ Step 4 is where marks are lost

Students draw the hyperbola missing the origin. Object at the pole/optical centre gives image at the pole/optical centre — so (0, 0) is always on the curve, for all four cases.

Slide 10 · The algebra behind the picture

Why the asymptotes land exactly there

Lens

v = f uu + f

  • u → ∞ ⟹ v → +f  (horizontal asymptote)
  • u → −f ⟹ denominator → 0 ⟹ v → ±∞  (vertical asymptote)
  • u = 0 ⟹ v = 0 ✓ passes through the origin

(u+f)(v−f) = −f²

Rectangular hyperbola, new origin (−f, +f), symmetric about the line v = −u.

dv/du = f²/(u+f)² > 0

⟹ every branch of a LENS curve RISES left-to-right.

Mirror

v = f uu − f

  • u → ∞ ⟹ v → f  (horizontal asymptote)
  • u → f ⟹ v → ±∞  (vertical asymptote)
  • u = 0 ⟹ v = 0 ✓ passes through the origin

(u−f)(v−f) = +f²

Rectangular hyperbola, new origin (f, f), symmetric about the line v = u.

dv/du = − f²/(u−f)² < 0

⟹ every branch of a MIRROR curve FALLS left-to-right.

One-line symmetry that kills the whole topic

Replace u → −u in the lens equation and you get the mirror equation exactly. The mirror's v–u graph is the lens's v–u graph reflected in the v-axis — same |f|. Use the Overlay switch in the builder to see it.

What the curve is telling you

Read the graph like a topper

Convex lens — the part of the curve left of u = −f is a real, inverted image; right of it is virtual, erect.
Concave mirror — same story, but the branches fall and both asymptotes sit at f (negative).

Object inside focus

The branch on the opposite side of the vertical asymptote from the “usual” one — i.e. between u = −f and 0 for a convex lens — is the virtual, erect, magnified branch. Its v is negative and runs off to −∞ as u → −f.

Where |m| = 1

The curve crosses the line v = −u (lens) or v = u (mirror) at u = 2f, v = 2f. That point is m = −1: real, inverted, same size. It is the fastest way to read f off an unlabelled graph.

Sign of v = nature

Lens: v > 0 ⟹ real (other side), v < 0 ⟹ virtual. Mirror: v < 0 ⟹ real (in front), v > 0 ⟹ virtual (behind). Sign of m gives erect/inverted.

The other two graphs JEE asks for

Companion graphs: 1/v vs 1/u and m vs u

1/v vs 1/u — a straight line. Lens: slope +1, intercept 1/f. Mirror: slope −1, intercept 1/f. Read f straight off the intercept.
m vs u — lens: m = f/(u+f), mirror: m = f/(f−u). Vertical asymptote at the focus, horizontal asymptote m = 0, and m = +1 at u = 0, m = −1 at u = 2f.

Why the straight line is the exam favourite

Lens: 1/v = 1/u + 1/f ⟹ y = x + c, slope +1, y-intercept 1/f, x-intercept −1/f.
Mirror: 1/v = −1/u + 1/f ⟹ slope −1, same y-intercept 1/f.
A slope of +1 vs −1 instantly tells you lens vs mirror — no curve-reading needed.

m–u graph landmarks (convex lens, f > 0)

  • u → −∞ ⟹ m → 0⁻ (highly diminished, inverted)
  • u = −2f ⟹ m = −1
  • u → −f⁻ ⟹ m → −∞;  u → −f⁺ ⟹ m → +∞ (sign flips!)
  • u = 0 ⟹ m = +1
  • |m| > 1 region ⟹ magnified; between −f and 0 it is virtual + magnified

Where the marks go

Seven traps — all of them have appeared in JEE

⚠ 1 · Missing the origin

The curve passes through (0, 0) — object at the pole/optical centre gives the image at the pole/optical centre. Most students draw the hyperbola skipping it.

⚠ 2 · Wrong slope on the branches

Lens branches rise ((u+f)(v−f) = −f²). Mirror branches fall ((u−f)(v−f) = +f²). Drawing a lens curve with falling branches is the #1 sketch error.

⚠ 3 · Swapping the asymptotes

Lens: vertical at u = −f, horizontal at v = +f. Mirror: both at f. The lens new-origin is (−f, f); the mirror's is (f, f).

⚠ 4 · Concave lens “escape”

For a concave lens the horizontal asymptote is v = −|f|: the image can never be farther than F from the lens, and is always virtual, erect, diminished.

⚠ 5 · The magnification sign

m = v/u for lenses but m = −v/u for mirrors. Forgetting the minus flips erect/inverted in every mirror answer.

⚠ 6 · Mis-reading 1/v–1/u

Intercept = 1/f, not f. Slope +1 = lens, −1 = mirror. Students quote the intercept as f directly and lose the sign too.

⚠ 7 · Marking (f, f) on a lens curve

The point (f, f) is the mirror's new origin, not a point on the lens curve. On a lens curve the special point is (−2f, +2f) where m = −1. Also: v–u is NOT a straight line — only 1/v–1/u is.

Universal truths

All four curves are rectangular hyperbolas passing through (0, 0), with asymptotes parallel to the axes. The asymptote crossing is the new origin.

Fastest identification

Branches rising ⟹ lens. Branches falling ⟹ mirror. Then read the sign of the new origin to get the sign of f.

Companion graphs

1/v–1/u: straight line, slope +1 (lens) / −1 (mirror), intercept 1/f.
m–u: hyperbola, m = +1 at u = 0, m = −1 at u = 2f.

Mastering Logarithms

A complete journey — from the very first definition to the powerful applications you'll meet in Class 11 & 12 Physics

$\log_b(x) = y \iff b^y = x$

1 Why Do Logarithms Exist?

Imagine you are a 17th-century astronomer trying to multiply two enormous numbers by hand. Multiplication is slow and error-prone.

But what if you could turn multiplication into addition?

That's exactly what John Napier did in 1614 when he invented logarithms.

The Big Idea: A logarithm answers the question — "To what power must I raise this base to get that number?"

Today, logarithms appear everywhere: earthquakes, sound, acidity, radioactive decay, and even in how our ears perceive loudness.

2 The Definition

For any positive base $b \neq 1$ and any positive number $x$:

$\log_b(x) = y \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad b^y = x$

Worked Examples

$\log_2(8) = 3$   because   $2^3 = 8$
$\log_3(81) = 4$   because   $3^4 = 81$
$\log_5\!\left(\frac{1}{25}\right) = -2$   because   $5^{-2} = \frac{1}{25}$

3 Two Golden Rules

Rule 1 $\log_b(b) = 1$   because   $b^1 = b$
Rule 2 $\log_b(1) = 0$   because   $b^0 = 1$
⚠️ Important: The base $b$ must be positive and $b \neq 1$. The argument $x$ must be strictly positive.

4 Fundamental Properties

Product $\log_b(xy) = \log_b(x) + \log_b(y)$
Quotient $\log_b\!\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right) = \log_b(x) - \log_b(y)$
Power $\log_b(x^n) = n\,\log_b(x)$
Change of Base $\log_b(x) = \dfrac{\log_k(x)}{\log_k(b)}$

5 Common Log & Natural Log

Common Log

$\log_{10}(x)$ or $\log(x)$

Base 10 — used in engineering and older physics books

Natural Log

$\ln(x) = \log_e(x)$

Base $e \approx 2.718$ — appears everywhere in physics!

ExpressionValue
$\ln(2)$≈ 0.693
$\ln(10)$≈ 2.303
$\log_{10}(2)$≈ 0.3010

6 Logarithms in Physics

🔬 Radioactive Decay

The number of undecayed nuclei at time $t$:

$N(t) = N_0 \, e^{-\lambda t}$

The half-life $T_{1/2}$:

$T_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \dfrac{0.693}{\lambda}$

🔊 Sound Intensity & Decibels

$\beta = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\dfrac{I}{I_0}\right)$

If intensity increases by 100×, sound level increases by 20 dB

7 More Physics Applications

⚡ RC Circuit Discharge

$V(t) = V_0 \, e^{-t/RC}$

Time to drop to fraction $f$: $t = -RC \cdot \ln(f)$

🌡️ Newton's Law of Cooling

$T(t) = T_s + (T_0 - T_s)\,e^{-kt}$

Solving for time: $t = -\dfrac{1}{k}\ln\!\left(\dfrac{T - T_s}{T_0 - T_s}\right)$

8 Practice Problems

Q1. Evaluate $\log_3(27) + \log_3(9) - \log_3(1)$. Easy
$\log_3(27) = 3$, $\log_3(9) = 2$, $\log_3(1) = 0$.
Sum = $3 + 2 - 0 = \boxed{5}$
Q2. If $\log_{10}(2) = 0.3010$, find $\log_{10}(5)$. Easy
$\log_{10}(5) = \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{10}{2}\right) = 1 - 0.3010 = \boxed{0.6990}$

9 Physics Problems

Q3. Half-life is 5 years. What fraction remains after 20 years? Medium
Number of half-lives = $20/5 = 4$.
Fraction = $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^4 = \boxed{\frac{1}{16}} \approx 6.25\%$
Q4. By how many dB does sound level increase if intensity becomes 1000× greater? Medium
$\Delta\beta = 10\log_{10}(1000) = 10 \times 3 = \boxed{30 \text{ dB}}$

10 Quick-Reference Cheat Sheet

📌 Core Identities

  • $\log_b(b^x) = x$   and   $b^{\log_b x} = x$
  • $\log_b(1) = 0$   and   $\log_b(b) = 1$
  • $\log_b(xy) = \log_b x + \log_b y$
  • $\log_b(x/y) = \log_b x - \log_b y$
  • $\log_b(x^n) = n\log_b x$

📌 Physics Formulas

  • Radioactive decay: $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$,   $T_{1/2} = \dfrac{0.693}{\lambda}$
  • Decibels: $\beta = 10\log_{10}(I/I_0)$
  • RC discharge: $V = V_0 e^{-t/RC}$
  • Newton cooling: $T = T_s + (T_0 - T_s)e^{-kt}$

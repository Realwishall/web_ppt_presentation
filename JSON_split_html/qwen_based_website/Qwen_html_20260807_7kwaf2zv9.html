THE REASONING

Why does swapping the order flip the direction?

01

Two vectors in, one vector out

The cross product of a and b produces a new vector perpendicular to both — it sticks out of the plane they span. Its length equals the area of that parallelogram (the shaded sheet in the scene). So far, nothing depends on order…

02

…but the direction is decided by the right hand

Of the two perpendicular directions, nature picks one using the right-hand rule: curl your fingers from the first vector toward the second; your thumb is the answer. If you swap the order, the curl runs the opposite way around the plane — so the thumb points exactly opposite.

03

Same length, opposite sign

That's why a×b and b×a always have equal magnitude and an angle of 180° between them — verify it live in the readout. Edge case: if the vectors are parallel, the area is zero and both products collapse into the zero vector, which has no direction at all (try the “Parallel” preset).

04

The algebra agrees

Written as a determinant, swapping the order of the vectors means swapping two rows — and swapping two rows of a determinant negates it:

ijk a₁a₂a₃ b₁b₂b₃
— swap rows ⇄ —
ijk b₁b₂b₃ a₁a₂a₃
= −(first)

So b×a = −(a×b). The cross product is anti-commutative: order doesn't just matter — reversing the order reverses the answer.

a × b  =  − ( b × a )

Same magnitude. Opposite direction. Every time.

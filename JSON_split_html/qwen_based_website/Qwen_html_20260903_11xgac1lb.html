1 Why Do Logarithms Exist?

Imagine you are a 17th-century astronomer trying to multiply two enormous numbers by hand. Multiplication is slow and error-prone. But what if you could turn multiplication into addition?

That's exactly what John Napier did in 1614 when he invented logarithms. The word itself comes from Greek: logos (ratio) + arithmos (number).

The Big Idea: A logarithm answers the question — "To what power must I raise this base to get that number?"

Today, logarithms appear everywhere: measuring earthquakes (Richter scale), sound (decibels), acidity (pH), radioactive decay, capacitor discharge, and even in the way our ears perceive loudness. They are the bridge between the multiplicative world and the additive world.

2 The Definition

For any positive base $b \neq 1$ and any positive number $x$, the logarithm of $x$ to base $b$ is the exponent $y$ such that:

$\log_b(x) = y \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad b^y = x$

Reading the notation

In $\log_b(x)$:

  • $b$ is called the base
  • $x$ is called the argument (must be positive!)
  • The whole expression gives you the exponent

Worked Examples

$\log_2(8) = ?$   →   We ask: $2^? = 8$. Since $2^3 = 8$, the answer is $\boxed{3}$.
$\log_3(81) = ?$   →   $3^? = 81$. Since $3^4 = 81$, the answer is $\boxed{4}$.
$\log_5\!\left(\frac{1}{25}\right) = ?$   →   $5^? = \frac{1}{25} = 5^{-2}$, so the answer is $\boxed{-2}$.
$\log_{10}(1000) = ?$   →   $10^? = 1000 = 10^3$, so the answer is $\boxed{3}$.

Two Golden Rules (memorize these!)

Rule 1 $\log_b(b) = 1$   because   $b^1 = b$
Rule 2 $\log_b(1) = 0$   because   $b^0 = 1$

⚠️ Important Restrictions

  • The base $b$ must be positive and $b \neq 1$.
  • The argument $x$ must be strictly positive ($x > 0$).
  • $\log_b(0)$ is undefined.
  • $\log_b(\text{negative})$ is undefined (in real numbers).

3 The Fundamental Properties

These are the workhorses of every logarithmic calculation. Learn them, practice them, love them.

Product Rule $\log_b(xy) = \log_b(x) + \log_b(y)$

Log of a product = sum of logs. This is the property that made Napier famous — it turns multiplication into addition!

Quotient Rule $\log_b\!\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right) = \log_b(x) - \log_b(y)$

Log of a quotient = difference of logs.

Power Rule $\log_b(x^n) = n\,\log_b(x)$

Exponents come out front as multipliers. Extremely useful in physics!

Change of Base $\log_b(x) = \dfrac{\log_k(x)}{\log_k(b)}$

Convert any log to any other base. Usually we convert to base 10 or base $e$ for calculator use.

Reciprocal $\log_b(x) = \dfrac{1}{\log_x(b)}$
Base Switch $b^{\log_b(x)} = x$   and   $\log_b(b^x) = x$

Putting them together — a worked simplification

Simplify $\log_2(8) + \log_2(4) - \log_2(2)$.
Using the rules: $= \log_2\!\left(\dfrac{8 \times 4}{2}\right) = \log_2(16) = \boxed{4}$.
If $\log_{10}(2) = 0.3010$, find $\log_{10}(8)$.
$\log_{10}(8) = \log_{10}(2^3) = 3\log_{10}(2) = 3 \times 0.3010 = \boxed{0.9030}$.

4 Common Log & Natural Log

Common Logarithm — base 10

Written as $\log_{10}(x)$ or simply $\log(x)$ in many textbooks (especially in engineering and older physics books).

$\log_{10}(100) = 2$,   $\log_{10}(0.001) = -3$,   $\log_{10}(\sqrt{10}) = 0.5$.

Natural Logarithm — base $e$

Written as $\log_e(x)$ or $\ln(x)$. The base $e \approx 2.71828$ is one of the most important numbers in mathematics. It arises naturally whenever something grows or decays continuously.

The number $e$ is defined as $e = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n \approx 2.71828$.

In Class 11 & 12 Physics, you will see $\ln$ everywhere — in radioactive decay, capacitor discharge, Newton's law of cooling, and more. Get comfortable with it!

Useful numerical values to remember

ExpressionValue
$\ln(2)$≈ 0.693
$\ln(10)$≈ 2.303
$\log_{10}(2)$≈ 0.3010
$\log_{10}(e)$≈ 0.4343

🔗 Conversion between ln and log₁₀

$\ln(x) = 2.303 \times \log_{10}(x)$   and   $\log_{10}(x) = 0.4343 \times \ln(x)$

This factor $2.303$ shows up constantly in physics numericals!

5 The Logarithmic Function — Shape & Behavior

The function $f(x) = \log_b(x)$ is the inverse of the exponential function $g(x) = b^x$. Their graphs are mirror images across the line $y = x$.

Key features of $y = \log_b(x)$

  • Domain: $(0, \infty)$ — only positive inputs
  • Range: $(-\infty, \infty)$ — all real outputs
  • x-intercept: $(1, 0)$ — because $\log_b(1) = 0$
  • Vertical asymptote: $x = 0$ (the y-axis)
  • If $b > 1$: the function is increasing (slowly!)
  • If $0 < b < 1$: the function is decreasing

Why does it grow so slowly?

Logarithmic growth is the slowest kind of unbounded growth. To go from $\log_{10}(x) = 1$ to $\log_{10}(x) = 6$, $x$ has to grow from 10 to 1,000,000! This is why logarithms are perfect for compressing huge ranges — like earthquake energies or sound intensities — into small, manageable numbers.

6 Logarithms in Class 11 & 12 Physics

This is where everything comes together. Almost every "exponential" phenomenon in physics uses logarithms when we want to solve for time, ratios, or orders of magnitude.

🔬 6.1 — Radioactive Decay (Class 12)

The number of undecayed nuclei at time $t$:

$N(t) = N_0 \, e^{-\lambda t}$

where $\lambda$ is the decay constant. Taking natural log of both sides:

$\ln\!\left(\dfrac{N}{N_0}\right) = -\lambda t$

The half-life $T_{1/2}$ is the time for the sample to reduce to half:

$T_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \dfrac{0.693}{\lambda}$

This formula appears in nearly every board exam!

🔊 6.2 — Sound Intensity & Decibels

The sound level $\beta$ in decibels (dB) is defined as:

$\beta = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\dfrac{I}{I_0}\right)$

where $I_0 = 10^{-12}\,\text{W/m}^2$ is the threshold of hearing.

Example: If intensity increases by a factor of 100, the sound level increases by $10\log_{10}(100) = 20$ dB.

🧪 6.3 — pH Scale (Chemistry crossover)

$\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$

A solution with $[\text{H}^+] = 10^{-4}$ M has pH = 4 (acidic). Each unit change in pH means a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration.

🌍 6.4 — Richter Scale (Earthquakes)

$M = \log_{10}\!\left(\dfrac{A}{A_0}\right)$

An earthquake of magnitude 6 is 10 times stronger in amplitude (and about 31.6 times more energetic) than one of magnitude 5.

⚡ 6.5 — RC Circuit Discharge (Class 12)

When a charged capacitor discharges through a resistor, the voltage decays as:

$V(t) = V_0 \, e^{-t/RC}$

The time constant $\tau = RC$. To find the time for the voltage to drop to a fraction $f$ of its initial value:

$t = -RC \cdot \ln(f)$

🌡️ 6.6 — Newton's Law of Cooling (Class 11)

The temperature of a body cooling in a surrounding at temperature $T_s$:

$T(t) = T_s + (T_0 - T_s)\,e^{-kt}$

Solving for time:

$t = -\dfrac{1}{k}\ln\!\left(\dfrac{T - T_s}{T_0 - T_s}\right)$

💡 6.7 — Beer-Lambert Law (Absorption of Light)

$I = I_0 \, e^{-\mu x} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = -\dfrac{1}{\mu}\ln\!\left(\dfrac{I}{I_0}\right)$

Used to find how deep light penetrates a material.

📈 6.8 — Arrhenius Equation (Reaction Rates / Thermionics)

$k = A\,e^{-E_a/(RT)} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \ln k = \ln A - \dfrac{E_a}{RT}$

Taking logs converts the exponential into a straight line — perfect for plotting and finding activation energy.

7 Practice Problems

Try each problem first. Click "Show Solution" when you're ready.

Q1. Evaluate $\log_3(27) + \log_3(9) - \log_3(1)$. Easy
$\log_3(27) = 3$, $\log_3(9) = 2$, $\log_3(1) = 0$.
Sum = $3 + 2 - 0 = \boxed{5}$.
Alternatively: $\log_3\!\left(\frac{27 \times 9}{1}\right) = \log_3(243) = \log_3(3^5) = 5$. ✓
Q2. If $\log_{10}(2) = 0.3010$, find $\log_{10}(5)$. Easy
$\log_{10}(5) = \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{10}{2}\right) = \log_{10}(10) - \log_{10}(2) = 1 - 0.3010 = \boxed{0.6990}$.
Q3. Solve for $x$: $\log_2(x) + \log_2(x-2) = 3$. Medium
Combine: $\log_2(x(x-2)) = 3 \Rightarrow x(x-2) = 2^3 = 8$.
$x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0 \Rightarrow (x-4)(x+2) = 0$.
$x = 4$ or $x = -2$. But $x > 0$ and $x - 2 > 0$, so $x > 2$.
Therefore $x = \boxed{4}$.
Q4. A radioactive sample has a half-life of 5 years. What fraction remains after 20 years? Medium
Number of half-lives = $20/5 = 4$.
Fraction remaining = $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^4 = \boxed{\frac{1}{16}} \approx 6.25\%$.
Q5. The decay constant of a substance is $\lambda = 0.01\,\text{day}^{-1}$. Find its half-life. Medium
$T_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = \dfrac{0.693}{0.01} = \boxed{69.3 \text{ days}}$.
Q6. By how many decibels does the sound level increase if the intensity becomes 1000 times greater? Medium
$\Delta\beta = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{1000\,I}{I}\right) = 10\log_{10}(1000) = 10 \times 3 = \boxed{30 \text{ dB}}$.
Q7. A capacitor discharges through a resistor with time constant $\tau = RC = 2\,\text{s}$. How long until the voltage drops to 10% of its initial value? Hard
$V = V_0 e^{-t/\tau}$. Set $V/V_0 = 0.1$:
$0.1 = e^{-t/2} \Rightarrow \ln(0.1) = -t/2 \Rightarrow t = -2\ln(0.1)$.
$\ln(0.1) = -\ln(10) \approx -2.303$.
$t = 2 \times 2.303 = \boxed{4.606 \text{ s}}$.
Q8. A body cools from 80°C to 50°C in 10 minutes in a room at 20°C. Using Newton's law of cooling, find the cooling constant $k$. Hard
$T(t) = T_s + (T_0 - T_s)e^{-kt}$.
$50 = 20 + (80-20)e^{-10k} \Rightarrow 30 = 60\,e^{-10k}$.
$e^{-10k} = 0.5 \Rightarrow -10k = \ln(0.5) = -0.693$.
$k = \boxed{0.0693 \text{ min}^{-1}}$.
Q9. An earthquake of magnitude 7 releases how many times more energy than one of magnitude 5? (Use: Energy $\propto 10^{1.5M}$.) Hard
$\dfrac{E_7}{E_5} = \dfrac{10^{1.5 \times 7}}{10^{1.5 \times 5}} = 10^{1.5(7-5)} = 10^{3} = \boxed{1000}$.
A magnitude-7 quake releases about 1000 times more energy than a magnitude-5 quake!
Q10. Prove that $\log_a(b) \cdot \log_b(c) \cdot \log_c(a) = 1$. Hard
Using the change-of-base formula with natural logs:
$\log_a(b) = \dfrac{\ln b}{\ln a},\quad \log_b(c) = \dfrac{\ln c}{\ln b},\quad \log_c(a) = \dfrac{\ln a}{\ln c}$.
Product = $\dfrac{\ln b}{\ln a} \cdot \dfrac{\ln c}{\ln b} \cdot \dfrac{\ln a}{\ln c} = \boxed{1}$. ∎

8 Quick-Reference Cheat Sheet

📌 Core Identities

  • $\log_b(b^x) = x$   and   $b^{\log_b x} = x$
  • $\log_b(1) = 0$   and   $\log_b(b) = 1$
  • $\log_b(xy) = \log_b x + \log_b y$
  • $\log_b(x/y) = \log_b x - \log_b y$
  • $\log_b(x^n) = n\log_b x$
  • $\log_b x = \dfrac{\ln x}{\ln b}$

📌 Key Values

  • $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$   |   $\ln 10 \approx 2.303$
  • $\log_{10} 2 \approx 0.3010$   |   $\log_{10} e \approx 0.4343$

📌 Physics Formulas

  • Radioactive decay: $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$,   $T_{1/2} = \dfrac{0.693}{\lambda}$
  • Decibels: $\beta = 10\log_{10}(I/I_0)$
  • pH: $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$
  • RC discharge: $V = V_0 e^{-t/RC}$
  • Newton cooling: $T = T_s + (T_0 - T_s)e^{-kt}$

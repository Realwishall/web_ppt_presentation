total internal reflection
and the optical fibre

A ray leaving a denser medium has two fates, and each one bends it by a different amount. The larger bend is the one students forget.

90° − θclargest deviation if it refracts out
180° − 2θclargest deviation if it is reflected
μ ≥ √2slab that traps every incoming ray

The critical angle sets the switch

Light travelling from denser to rarer bends away from the normal. At one particular incidence the refracted ray grazes the surface.

μd sin θc = μr sin 90°
sin θc = μr / μd   (= 1/μ into air)
  • Beyond θc no refracted ray exists — all the energy returns.
  • TIR needs both conditions: denser → rarer and i > θc.
  • θc depends on wavelength, so violet has the smallest θc in glass.

Critical angles into air

Water, μ = 1.33   →   θc = 48.8°
Crown glass, μ = 1.50   →   θc = 41.8°
Flint glass, μ = 1.62   →   θc = 38.1°
Diamond, μ = 2.42   →   θc = 24.4°

Diamond's tiny θc means almost every ray that enters is trapped and bounced repeatedly before escaping — the reason a cut stone sparkles.

Two fates, two deviation formulas

Deviation is always the angle between the original direction of travel and the final direction.

It refracts out  (i < θc)

δ = r − i,   sin r = μ sin i

Both r and r − i grow with i. Push i right up to θc and r → 90°.

δmax = 90° − θc

Approached but never reached: at i = θc the refracted ray has already died.

It is totally reflected  (i ≥ θc)

δ = 180° − 2i

Now δ decreases as i grows, so the maximum sits at the smallest angle TIR allows.

δmax = 180° − 2θc

At grazing incidence (i → 90°) the ray skims back along the surface and δ → 0.

(180° − 2θc) − (90° − θc) = 90° − θc > 0   reflection always wins

Where the marks are lost

Asked for “maximum deviation of a ray inside the medium”, most students compute 90° − θc and stop. That is only the maximum of the refracted branch. The reflected branch reaches 180° − 2θc, which is always 90° − θc larger.

Read the stem carefully: “the ray that emerges” restricts you to refraction; “the ray” or “any ray” does not.

Same idea, other dressing

  • Mirage: hot air near the road is optically rarer, a grazing ray is totally reflected and you see sky on tarmac.
  • A diamond's brilliance, a crack in glass flashing silver, a water-filled pipe glowing at its end.
  • Prisms at 45° in binoculars and periscopes replace mirrors because TIR loses no light.

Trapped inside a glass block

A ray enters the top face at incidence i, refracts to r, then runs into a side face. Does it escape sideways or stay inside?

1
sin i = μ sin r

Snell's law at the top face.

2
angle at the side face = 90° − r

The two faces are perpendicular, so the normals are too.

3
90° − r ≥ θc  ⟹  cos r ≥ 1/μ

TIR condition, rewritten with sin θc = 1/μ.

4
cos r = √(1 − sin²i/μ²)  ⟹  μ² ≥ 1 + sin²i

Since sin i ≤ 1, a block with μ ≥ √2 ≈ 1.414 traps every ray, whatever the entry angle.

The fibre only keeps the shallow rays

A core of index μ₁ inside a cladding of slightly lower index μ₂. Every bounce at the core–cladding wall must clear the critical angle, so only rays entering within a cone stay inside. Drag the scene to rotate it.

3D view needs WebGL. The ray still obeys the same rule: it is guided only while the angle it makes with the wall's normal stays above θc, i.e. while sin(entry) ≤ NA.

Numerical aperture, derived

1
μ₀ sin θa = μ₁ sin θr

Refraction at the flat entry face, from the outside medium μ₀.

2
90° − θr ≥ θc,   sin θc = μ₂/μ₁

TIR at the wall; the wall's normal is perpendicular to the axis.

3
μ₀ sin θa ≤ √(μ₁² − μ₂²)
NA = √(μ₁² − μ₂²),   θa = sin⁻¹(NA/μ₀)

In air μ₀ = 1, so θa = sin⁻¹(NA). Note NA is a pure number and can exceed 1 on paper — that simply means every incident ray is accepted.

Standard fibre numbers

μ₁ = 1.50, μ₂ = 1.48
NA = √(2.25 − 2.1904) = √0.0596 = 0.244
θa = sin⁻¹(0.244) = 14.1°

Core diameter is of order 10⁻⁶ m, which is why the fibre must be fed by a laser or LED rather than a lamp, and why a tighter index contrast (smaller NA) is deliberately chosen — it limits how many paths a pulse can take and keeps it sharp over kilometres.

Two constant slips

θa is measured from the fibre axis, not from the face. And the θc in a fibre uses μ₂/μ₁, never 1/μ₁ — there is no air at that boundary.

Carry these five lines into the exam

sin θc = μrarer / μdenser
δmax (refracted) = 90° − θc
δmax (reflected) = 180° − 2θc
slab traps the ray if μ² ≥ 1 + sin²i
traps everything if μ ≥ √2
NA = √(μ₁² − μ₂²), θa = sin⁻¹(NA/μ₀)
